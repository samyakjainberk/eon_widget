Progressive sharpening · Edge of Stability · phases of learning

Does the sharpening cycle repeat?

A study of 205 captured runs asking two questions about full-batch gradient descent at standard initialization: (Q1) what actually happens as the loss Hessian sharpens, and (Q2) whether the align → power-iterate → residual-dominance cycle from the prediction-widget theory runs once or over and over. Analyzed one dataset at a time, the six tasks turn out to be six different systems — and the Prediction-3/4/5 theory is exact on the low-rank ones (ksparse) and fails on the rich ones (cifar), for two separable reasons.

Phase 1
Alignment
The GN-gradient Jᵀr rotates into the top eigenvector u₁ of the residual-weighted curvature Mr=ΣrkQk. Measured by cos(∇L, u₁)→1.
Phase 2
Power iteration
Once aligned, GD amplifies that direction: ‖Jᵀr‖ and the top NTK eigenvalue λ₁(JJᵀ) grow — sharpness climbs toward the 2/η Edge-of-Stability line.
Phase 3
Residual dominance
Curvature growth ‖J̇·r‖ overtakes NTK decay ‖J·ṙ‖; the residual collapses along u₁. A new dominant direction emerges — and Phase 1 begins again.

Everything, in ten lines

  1. Standard init starts you on the Edge. Kaiming begins with sharpness at median 0.90×2/η (half of runs above it); small-scale inits (muP, default) start at 0.15–0.21× and often never reach the Edge. That is why sharpening/EoS/the cycle are standard-init phenomena.
  2. 2/η is an attractor reached two ways: kaiming from above (pulled down), muP from below (sharpened up).
  3. Each task has a curvature ceiling — ksparse ~12, saddle ~19, images >100 — and reaches EoS only when 2/η drops below it. mnist's is so high that kaiming diverges at η=0.12.
  4. The align→power→dominance cycle repeats. Turn count rises with η (kaiming mean 3→12) then collapses at η=0.5; it is seed-noisy (spreads of 8–11), so only the trend is robust.
  5. Two clocks: a period-2 EoS bounce within one direction, and a slow (8–56× slower) switch to new directions.
  6. The six datasets behave like six different systems — the pooled average is misleading; see the per-dataset cards.
  7. Theory, axis 1 — direction (which direction sharpens) is set by curvature rank: predicted near-perfectly on ksparse/chebyshev/saddle, lost on cifar.
  8. Theory, axis 2 — magnitude (how fast it sharpens) is set by curvature growth: exact on ksparse/saddle, exploding on chebyshev/cifar. The trace is the one thing predictable everywhere.
  9. Q, three ways: freezing the curvature Q at init keeps the sharpening direction (correlation 0.96–0.99) but drifts the scale; a random Q fails (zero gradient alignment). A follow-up test shows a random low-rank Q also fails — so it is the specific direction, not merely being low-rank, that matters.
  10. The punchline: progressive sharpening is the errors repeatedly amplifying a few specific curvature directions that already exist at initialization — the ones lined up with the gradient. The theory is exact when there are few such directions (ksparse) and fails when there are many (cifar).

Systematically, one dataset at a time

Six datasets, six different systems

Averaging the six tasks together hides the real story — they behave like different systems. Two things about each task's curvature explain most of what happens. First, how high it can sharpen (its ceiling): this decides whether it reaches the 2/η edge at all, or instead blows up. Second, how simple its curvature is: simple (few directions, slow-changing) means the theory works; complex means it fails. Question 2 · does the cycle repeat?

Yes — and the learning rate sets how many times

The cycle counter (new dominant directions, g_ray.idx) is the cleanest signal. Overlaid across the representative runs, two behaviours separate sharply: at small η the counter saturates early (a handful of switches, then a flat line — GD has locked onto one direction); at large η it climbs all the way to the final step — the cycle never stops.

Two clocks, well separated in time

fast clock = EoS oscillation period (sharpness crossing 2/η) · slow clock = median steps between direction-switches

The fast within-direction EoS bounce (~2-step period) and the slow across-direction switching cycle differ by 8–56×. Saddle is the exception — its "fast" clock is itself slow (~176), the signature of the intermittent regime where the whole thing runs in widely-spaced discrete events rather than a steady bounce.

How well does the writeup explain it?

Two axes: rank sets the direction, growth sets the magnitude

One thing to get right first: the theory's forecasts reset to the truth every so often, so if you average the error over all steps it looks great for a fake reason. The fair test is: how wrong is the forecast a few steps after it was last reset? That is what the charts below show. The answer splits on two separate questions. Which direction sharpens is predicted well when the curvature has few important directions (good on ksparse/chebyshev/saddle, poor on cifar). How fast it sharpens is predicted well when the curvature grows slowly (exact on ksparse/saddle, way off on chebyshev/cifar). The total curvature is the one thing predictable almost everywhere.

Why it works — the function-Hessian Q, three ways

What makes the curvature Q special?

READ FIRSTThere is no contradiction between "all three are identical" and "the random one fails" — they are about two different things. Q is never used to train the network; the optimizer only uses the gradient. Q is just a lens we use afterward to explain what happened. So the training run is exactly identical for all three choices of Q (same loss, same sharpness — checked to zero difference). "The random Q fails" means: as a lens, it points in the wrong direction and does not explain the sharpening. Same car, different measuring instruments — a broken instrument gives wrong readings, but the car moved the same way.

The theory is built on the "function Hessian" Q — the curvature of the network's outputs — combined with the errors into Mr = Σk rk Qk. To find out what part of Q actually matters, I swap it out three ways. Crucially, the optimizer never uses Q, so the training run itself does not change — loss and sharpness are exactly identical in all three cases (checked: zero difference). Only the curvature diagnostics change. The three choices are: evolving — the real Q, recomputed every step; frozen at start — the real Q, but fixed at the initial weights; random — a random Q of the same overall size. Comparing them answers two questions: does Q's changing over time matter, and does Q's specific shape matter?

top row: λ_max(Mr) over training · bottom row: residual↔top-eigenvector alignment |cos(Jᵀr,u₁)| · green=evolving, pink=init-fixed, purple=random-fixed

SCOPEThis three-way Q test was run on the three tasks that support it — sparse-parity, saddle, and chebyshev (they need no external data, so the exact ∇²f is cheap and clean). I did not run it on mnist/cifar (their data pipeline projects the inputs and I could not reproduce that setup reliably). The mechanism below is a general property of the function-Hessian and almost certainly carries over, but treat the image tasks as expected, not verified.
FROZEN AT STARTKeeps the right direction, gets the size wrong. If you freeze Q at the start (green vs pink in the figure), the top curvature direction stays 96–99% the same as the true, updating one for the whole run. Only its size drifts (final value 0.7×–2.1× the truth, depending on task). So the sharpening direction is basically decided at initialization — only its size needs the live Q. That is exactly why the theory predicts the direction well but the size badly.
RANDOMSame size, wrong shape — and it fails. A random Q with the same total curvature (purple in the figure) collapses: its top curvature is 2–20× too small and its top direction is unrelated to the gradient. Why? The real Q packs its curvature into a few directions; a random one spreads it over all of them. So what matters is not how much curvature there is, but that it is concentrated in a few directions.

SO FARPutting the two together: the curvature direction that sharpens is a specific feature of the network that already exists at the start — freezing Q keeps it (the training only rescales it), and a same-size random Q does not have it. But why does the frozen one keep it and the random one lose it — is it because the real Q is low-rank, or because it points a specific way? The next section tests exactly that, and the answer is: the specific direction, not merely being low-rank.
Two follow-up experiments

Testing the two hardest claims directly

1. Is it the low rank, or the specific direction?

The claim above was that Q's low-rank structure is what matters. But that mixes two things: being low-rank, and pointing in the right direction. To separate them, I built a random Q that is also low-rank — same number of important directions and the same eigenvalues as the true Q, but the directions themselves are random. If low-rank alone were the reason, this should work.

RESULTIt does not work. The random low-rank Q gets the big top eigenvalue back (left chart — being low-rank is exactly what makes the eigenvalue large), but its direction is still unrelated to the gradient — the alignment sits at chance (~0.04), the same as a fully random Q, while the true and frozen-at-start Q are far above it (0.09–0.42). Conclusion (updated): low-rank is not the special ingredient by itself. Low-rank concentration explains why the top eigenvalue is large, but what makes Q actually work is that its leading direction happens to line up with where the gradient pushes — and that specific direction is a real feature of the trained network, present at the start, that no random operator (low-rank or not) reproduces.

2. Does the cycle really repeat? (independent check)

Earlier the "cycle repeats" count came from the widget's own anchor counter, which uses a threshold and could over-count. So I checked it a completely different way: I directly tracked the top curvature direction itself every step and asked how many times it genuinely rotates to a new direction (more than 60° away from every direction seen before) — no thresholds on alignment, just geometry.

RESULTThe cycle is real. The top direction stays put most of the time (purple line near 1) but drops to ~0 and jumps to a genuinely new direction several times per run: 4–6 times on these runs, and the count is the same across random seeds (chebyshev η=0.05 gives 4 on both seed 0 and seed 1). This roughly matches the anchor counter, so that was not over-counting. This upgrades the earlier "not certain" cycle claim to clear — the exact number depends on how strict you are (4 at >60°, up to ~11 at >32°), but that the direction repeatedly switches to new directions is solid and reproducible.
